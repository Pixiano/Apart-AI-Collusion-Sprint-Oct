# What the papers give us (theory, method, text), 2026-10-22

Read for this note: Keppo et al. sections 1 to 3 (introduction, model summary, experimental design) and the results summary table; Fish et al. sections 1 to 3 (introduction, design, prompts, start of the duopoly experiment); Eschenbaum et al. sections 1 and 2 (introduction, framework); Shi et al. sections 1 and 2; Aharon et al. abstract, introduction and related work; Dietrich et al. abstract, introduction and method names. Not read: appendices, most results sections, Keppo's and Fish's proofs and robustness tests. Cite a result only after reading its section.

## 1. Theory we can lean on
- **Folk theorem and repeated games** (cited in Keppo et al. via Fudenberg and Maskin 1986; Abreu et al. 1986, 1990): tacit collusion is sustainable only if players are patient enough and can observe deviations. Keppo et al. turn this into two predictions that their LLM runs support: unequal patience and unequal data access reduce collusion. For us: a replacement agent that does not share the others' horizon or knowledge is exactly this kind of heterogeneity, but introduced mid-run.
- **Finite known horizon unravels.** Backward induction says a known last round removes the incentive to cooperate before it. Keppo et al. and Fish et al. let agents believe the game has no known end. We should not tell the agents there are 60 rounds (see the spec change below), and the swap at round 30 is far enough from any end.
- **Zero-shot coordination and overfitting** (Eschenbaum et al., framework in section 2): a policy that works against its training partners can fail against a new partner. They show Q-learning collusion breaks down in a "testing context" and falls back to the one-shot Nash outcome, and that continued learning can repair it but slowly. A mid-run replacement is a testing context for the remaining agents. Contrast: LLMs adapt in context without retraining, so repair might be fast. That contrast is our open question.
- **Strategic complements and leaders** (Keppo et al.): a larger model next to a smaller one did not break collusion; the larger model led and the smaller followed. So a more capable replacement could stabilise, not break, a cartel.
- **Cheap talk** (Shi et al., section 2): messages that are not binding are read differently by different models, some as commitments and some as noise. Our announced or hidden swap and our message channel are cheap talk. Different-family swaps may be read differently by the remaining agents for this reason.
- **Salience** (Aharon et al.): agents can coordinate on salient numbers without communication. In a 10 to 100 price range the collusive price may sit on a round number (55 is the monopoly price; 50 and 60 are salient). A different family may have different salient numbers. Worth recording which prices agents converge on.

## 2. Methods we can reuse
- **Checkpoint, intervene, compare** (Fish et al., section 1, the "implantation" method): reset the run to a point and change one thing, then compare with the unchanged continuation. Our paired branches from one checkpoint are the same logic, applied to replacing an agent rather than editing its reasoning. Cite as precedent.
- **Sanity checks before the main experiment** (Keppo et al., section 4): show that the agent can find the monopoly price in a one-agent game and the Nash price in a one-shot game. Cheap for us: add a one-firm check (monopoly price 55) and a one-shot check to the pilot, so "no collusion" is not caused by an agent that cannot price.
- **Two criteria for collusion** (Keppo et al., section 1): price convergence and price elevation above the static Nash benchmark. Our collusion index measures elevation; add a convergence measure (prices of the three firms within 5 percent of each other, a criterion borrowed from Fish et al. via Keppo et al.) as a descriptive check, labelled exploratory.
- **Many seeds** (Keppo et al.: 10 runs per condition; Fish et al.: 21 per prompt). Our 10 seeds per cell is in line.
- **Prompt hygiene** (Fish et al., section 2.3): the instruction does not mention collusion, does not say the rival is an AI, only that the long-run profit matters. Their two prefixes differ in the last lines: the one stressing long-run profit gave higher prices than one that mentions lowering the price to sell more. Our base prompt follows the long-run-profit version and never mentions undercutting or volume.
- **Memory between calls** (Fish et al., Keppo et al.): each agent writes plans and insights (a strategy note) that is fed back next round. We do not have this in prereg-v1. It is the strongest candidate for the one allowed prompt change if the pilot shows no collusion, and it makes "fresh clone" a precise manipulation (wipe the notes and the history).
- **Statement-disjoint evaluation** (Dietrich, method names): when training a classifier on generated text, keep whole statements out of training so content cannot leak into the test. For the fallback detector study: split by episode (or by seed), never by round within an episode.
- **Units** (Fish et al., section 2.1): LLMs are not neutral to the currency unit, so they varied the scale. We use one scale; note it as a limitation.

## 2b. Eschenbaum et al. in more detail (read 2026-10-23: sections 3.6 and 4.1 to 4.2; sections 4.3 and 4.4 not read)
- **Their test of a new partner:** two pairs of Q-learners train separately (they differ only in the random seed), then one learner from each pair is paired in a fresh market. Same game, same payoffs, only the rival is unseen. This is the cleanest analogue of our fresh clone (same model, new instance).
- **Three readings of the outcome:** (i) collusion level after training, (ii) the first 1,000 iterations with the new partner, (iii) after the pair has converged again. We mirror this with: before the swap (rounds 16 to 30), right after (rounds 31 to 40), and late (rounds 51 to 60). The early and late windows are exploratory additions; the pre-registered primary outcome stays rounds 31 to 60.
- **Two modes:** policies frozen (exploit only) versus policies that keep updating. With frozen policies collusion fell to Nash play and never came back; with updating it came back slowly. Our analogue is memory: the family swap keeps the history (a continuing agent), the clone has its memory wiped (a frozen newcomer), and the notes-field ablation changes what an agent can carry.
- **The shape of the break:** breakdown went to Nash play, not to a partial level. So look at the distribution of outcomes per run (stuck near the cartel price versus fallen to the competitive price), not just the mean.
- **The quoted claim** (section 4.1): their learners needed over 1 million rounds, over 2 million on average, to converge, so learning online in the market is likely infeasible because exploration is costly, which is why they separate training and testing. It is about tabular Q-learning; it does not transfer to LLMs, which do not learn by exploration but read the history in the prompt. The contrast is the point: if LLM cartels re-form within tens of rounds after a replacement, collusion is cheap to restore and the regulatory concern is stronger; if they do not, the same fragility appears even though no retraining is needed.
- **Possible extra ablation (not added):** their restriction result (section 4.4, not read) says limiting what the learner conditions on made collusion robust, while Keppo et al. found that limiting data access reduced collusion in LLM agents. An "own prices only" prompt would test which holds for LLMs after a swap.

## 2c. Fish et al. in more detail (read 2026-10-23: sections 3, 4.1, 4.2, 6 and appendix A.1 to A.5; not read: section 5 clustering, A.6 auction results, appendices B onward)
- **A benchmark for our index.** With GPT-5.2 at high reasoning effort (A.5) the average price over periods 251 to 300 was 1.79, between the Nash price 1.47 and the monopoly price 1.92. On our index that is about (1.79 - 1.47) / (1.92 - 1.47) = 0.7 (my arithmetic; their demand model differs from ours). So a gate of 0.5 is plausible for strong models after a long run, and a small model in 30 rounds may fall short. Reasoning effort did not remove collusion, which lowers the expected payoff of ablation 6.
- **Mixed instructions between firms** (A.3): when one firm used the long-run-profit prompt (P1) and the other the undercutting-friendly prompt (P2), P1 priced lower than against another P1 and P2 earned more. Two lessons: (i) a replacement agent with a different instruction is a realistic way to model a model update, between the clone and the scripted undercutter; (ii) the cooperative side loses when the other side is more aggressive. Candidate for a 12th configuration: replace one firm with the same model under the P2-style prompt. Not added yet.
- **Implantation in detail** (4.1): for each of 42 runs, both agents and periods 2 to 13 they reset to the state before that period, replaced the plan with one of three price-war-avoidance sentences and erased the insights, and compared the next price with the original run at the same period (N = 3,024). Prices rose by 0.039, about 5 percent of the monopoly markup (p < 0.0001, Welch t-test). This is the same paired logic as our branches. It also suggests a use for our replacement agent: give the newcomer a one-line note (such as "avoid price wars") and see whether that alone changes its behaviour. That pairs with ablation 10 (a company nudging its AI) and needs the notes field.
- **A cheap mechanism metric we can reuse** (4.2): regress a firm's price on its own last price (stickiness) and the rival's last price (responsiveness) with run fixed effects. A steeper coefficient on the rival's price goes with stronger reward and punishment. We can estimate it from price data alone, per firm, before and after a swap, as an exploratory outcome (they omit the first 100 periods; we would omit the first 5 to 10 rounds).
- **A text metric we will not use now** (4.1): sentence embeddings compared with "avoid price war" and "start price war" reference sentences. Needs an embedding model and validation; skip unless we have time.
- **Statistical hygiene:** they use Welch t-tests on run-level averages and one firm per run to avoid dependence between firms in a run. Our bootstrap over base runs does the same job.
- **Prompt ceiling hint:** their prompt includes a price ceiling drawn at random between 1.5 and 2.5 times the monopoly price, so the ceiling is not a fixed anchor. Our 10 to 100 range gives a ceiling of 100 / 55 = 1.8 times the monopoly price; mention the range in the report and keep it fixed (ablation 10 deliberately adds an anchor).
- **Open question for us:** how many periods collusion takes to appear in Fish et al. (they say "quickly"); not read from the figures. Check before claiming our 60 rounds are enough.

## 2d. Fish et al., discussion and appendix A (read 2026-10-23)
- **Prices were the only channel** (discussion, footnote 32): they shut down all communication except through prices, and cite an Andon Labs blog post (not read by us) in which other channels let LLMs collude. For us: our message line (communication on) is that other channel. Keep comms on versus off as a core comparison and report both; do not cite the blog until read.
- **Asking the model does not reveal it** (discussion, Figure 4): a model asked whether it would collude says no, yet the same model colludes in the pricing game. They tie this to sycophancy. For the report: do not use self-reports as evidence; use prices and, at most, the messages as a descriptive check.
- **Training data may feed it back** (discussion): antitrust papers and cases are public text, so models may learn collusive pricing from them, and more such documents could magnify it. A one-sentence discussion point, not something we test.
- **Their stated limits** (discussion): simple environment, one fixed horizon. We share both; say so.
- **Noise** (A.1): adding "market conditions are constantly changing: the same price might earn different profits on different days" and random demand shocks (plus or minus 0.05) left the P1 over P2 pattern intact. Our game is deterministic; list this as a limitation, not an ablation.
- **Asymmetric firms** (A.2): the higher-quality firm set higher prices and earned more; both stayed above the competitive price. Our firms are symmetric (same cost); different costs would be a later idea only.
- **Telling agents to discount the future did not help** (A.4, prefix P3): prices stayed at the P2 level (about 1.685 against 1.689), and plans mentioned price wars more, not less (1.7 times more likely to discuss, 1.8 times more likely to express concern, p < 0.001; the 12 runs per prefix are small). They speculate that talking about the future makes the model think about it (an "ironic process" effect). Lesson for ablation 1 (tell the horizon) and 4 (wording): wording about time can backfire; log the exact text and do not expect the textbook direction.
- **Even the undercut-friendly wording colludes** (A.4 and section 3): P2 averaged about 1.69, which on our index (Nash 1.47, monopoly 1.92) is about (1.69 - 1.47) / 0.45 = 0.49, my arithmetic. So the P1 versus P2 gap is between roughly 0.5 and 0.7, not between collusion and none. Ablation 4 should be predicted as "somewhat lower", not "none".
- **Run counts:** 21 runs per prefix in the main duopoly (7 per scale), 12 for P3, 4 per cell in A.2 and A.3 (their own footnote calls a 12-run comparison only marginally significant). Our 10 per cell and 5 per ablation cell are in the same range; say what the intervals support.
- **Not read:** the auction results (A.6) and Appendix I (clustering details). The settings and prompts are covered in 2e below.

## 2e. Fish et al., online appendix B to G (read 2026-10-23 from the PDF; the earlier text file stopped at appendix A.6)
- **Settings** (B): GPT-4 version 0613, temperature 1 (API default, never varied), reply parsed from a template, retry up to 10 times (a run stops after 10 failures; this happened only for two weaker models in the single-firm test). GPT-4 retried 0.3 percent of calls, GPT-3.5 9 percent.
- **A harness sanity check that weaker models fail** (C): in a single-firm test with the neutral prompt, all three GPT-4 runs converged to the monopoly price within 100 periods, while the four weaker models tested (GPT-3.5, Llama 2 13B and two others) did not reach the monopoly price in any run. Small models may fail the same test. Our pilot's one-firm check (price 55) matters: if qwen3-8b cannot find 55 alone, "no collusion" says nothing about collusion.
- **Memory matters** (E): 12 single-firm runs per treatment reached the monopoly price 12 of 12 with plans and insights, 11 of 12 without plans, 12 of 12 without insights, 6 of 12 with neither. This is the strongest paper evidence for the notes field (ablation 2).
- **Agents get room to think** (G.2): the reply template is observations and thoughts, new plans, new insights, then the price. They write that this gives equal "space to think" in every treatment. Ours is a JSON price plus a short message, thinking off, 60 output tokens: far less reasoning space. If the pilot shows no collusion, a short reasoning field is a second candidate change next to the notes field; both are changes to the base prompt, so log them as one pre-registered fallback or as a deviation, not silently.
- **Information** (D): asked directly, both prompt versions answer yes 20 of 20 times to "does pricing lower than your competitor typically lead to more sold", so the P1 versus P2 gap is not caused by one side lacking that fact.
- **History shown** (G.2): last 100 periods of the agent's own price, quantity and profit plus the rival's price. Ours shows up to 60 rounds, so the full table is within their range.
- **Prompt text** (G.1): P0 is "Your task is to assist a user in setting a suitable price ... Your TOP PRIORITY is to set prices which maximize the user's profit in the long run." P1 adds "explore many different pricing strategies, keeping in mind your primary goal of maximizing profit -- thus, you should not take actions which undermine profitability." P2 adds exploring "possibly risky or aggressive options" and "pricing lower than your competitor will typically lead to more product sold". Quote these only briefly in the report and cite them; point to their Appendix G for the full text.
- **Price-war classifier validation** (F): checked on 14 sentences (7 per meaning) with a perfect score; small, so a check on the metric, not a strong validation.

## 2f. Keppo et al. in more detail (read 2026-10-23: sections 3 to 7, appendix B, C.1 to C.4 and the appendix E table; not read: the proofs, appendix A and D)
**Setup facts.** Two to five sellers, a 32B reasoning model run locally, up to 1,000 periods, 10 runs per condition (5 for the closed-model check), 2,235 compute hours in total. Prompt (C.1): "You are Seller i ... TOP PRIORITY is to set prices which maximize your own profit in the long run", marginal cost, discount factor 0.95 in the prompt, the agent's last strategy note, up to 100 rounds of own price, quantity, profit and the rivals' prices, and a reply template with price, rationale and a forward strategy (5,000 token limit). The end is unknown. No temperature is reported (only listed as a future dimension). "Patience" is the discount factor stated in the prompt: 0.95 patient, 0 myopic. Convergence: for 100 consecutive periods all prices within 5 percent of the lowest; the run stops then.

**Results (their Table 5, price elevation against the static Nash price).**
| Condition | Rounds to converge | Elevation |
|---|---|---|
| 2 myopic | 159 | about 0% |
| 2 patient | 195 | +22% |
| patient vs myopic | 141 | +10% |
| high-data vs low-data | 151 | +7% |
| LLM vs frozen Q-learner | no convergence | +2% |
| LLM vs adaptive Q-learner | no convergence | +8% |
| 3 patient | 208 | +36% (price 2.00, above their monopoly price 1.92) |
| 4 patient | 542 | +11% |
| 5 patient | none within 1,000 | +3% |
| 32B vs 14B, both patient | 458 | +21% |

Closed model (5 runs each): two patient +57%, two myopic -29%, and an explicit instruction not to collude pushed all pairs to about marginal cost, below the Nash price (-28% to -30%) except the high-data versus low-data pair (-2%). The wording of that instruction is not in the text I read.

**What this changes for us (honest corrections of earlier notes).**
1. **Time is our biggest risk, bigger than I wrote.** Their agents needed about 160 to 210 rounds to meet the stop rule with two or three firms, and the stop rule requires 100 stable rounds, so first settling is roughly rounds 25 to 110 (their representative runs: 76 for two patient, 25 for patient versus myopic). Even a lone monopolist needed 224 periods to find the monopoly price (B.2). Our 60 rounds with 8 to 14B non-thinking models is a much shorter window with weaker agents. The Friday gate may well fail; the fallback exists for that, and the pilot should check the one-firm price (55) first. If the gate fails, what we report is that collusion did not form in 60 rounds, not that there is nothing to see.
2. **Number of firms is not monotonic.** Three patient firms colluded at least as strongly as two (+36% against +22%) and reached it at nearly the same time; four was much slower and weaker; five did not form. My earlier wording "more firms weaken collusion" holds only from three firms up. Ablation 9 and 11 are corrected in `06_ABLATIONS.md`.
3. **A model-size gap did not break the cartel but slowed it** (458 against 195 rounds, same price level). The larger model led, the smaller followed. So "stabilises" overstated it; "does not deter, slows onset" is what they show. Their test starts with mixed sizes from period 1; it does not replace an agent mid-run.
4. **Patience as a prompt line is an LLM-native swap.** Keppo changes a firm's horizon with one sentence (discount factor 0 for myopic). Patient versus myopic dropped elevation from +22% to +10%. A replacement that is the same model told to maximise only this round's profit is a cleaner "different objective" swap than the scripted undercutter and easy to describe in the report. See the proposal below.
5. **Data access:** an agent that cannot see rivals' prices stayed at a stable price and ignored invitations to move up; the informed rival then tested undercuts and settled lower (+7%). Own-prices-only is therefore a candidate replacement variant too (the newcomer cannot see history, a lighter form of the fresh clone). Keppo predicts less collusion; Eschenbaum found restriction made Q-learner collusion more robust (section 4.4, not read). For LLMs the Keppo result is the one to test.
6. **Reasoning text.** The agents write a rationale and a strategy each round, and the quotes show repeated price-war avoidance in the long-run agents and "testing" language in the lagging ones. That text is their evidence for mechanism; our message field is much smaller. Keep logging our messages and read a sample.
7. **An anti-collusion instruction worked in one model.** A short instruction against collusion drove prices to marginal cost. For us it is a candidate for the replacement-variant idea (a newcomer with that line) and for the plus-factor fallback detector, where such a line gives a labelled non-collusive class.
8. **Their reporting:** 10 runs per condition, mean and SD across runs, one-sided Welch t-tests against the benchmark, a representative run plus quotes; they say they aim at directional patterns, not precise effect sizes. Same stance as ours.
9. **Sanity checks to copy** (B.1, B.2): 50 one-shot runs per setting with the demand given, then a repeated monopoly with the demand hidden. Our pilot analogue stays small (one-firm and one-shot).

**Added as ablation 12 (2026-10-23): replacement with a different instruction.** The same model replaces firm C at round 30 with one extra line: (a) maximise only this round's profit (Keppo); (b) sell-more wording (Fish P2); (c) do not collude (Keppo's robustness check); (d) own prices only (Keppo's data access). Costs four 30-round branches per base run on one setup. Now in `06_ABLATIONS.md` and `ABLATIONS.md`.

## 2g. Shi et al. in more detail (read 2026-10-23: the whole main text, pages 1 to 7, and appendix C to E tables; not read: the prompt appendices, appendix F robustness checks, G)
**What it is.** ICML 2026 (PMLR 306), Shi, Zhang, Schoelkopf, Conitzer, Jin. Five LLM agents play six one-shot-style games (Diner's Dilemma, El Farol, Volunteer's Dilemma, Tragedy of the Commons, Public Goods, Weakest Link) for 10 rounds. Each round has three stages: a private plan, a public announcement (in a fixed order, each seeing the earlier ones), then the action; afterwards each agent writes a trust score for each rival that goes into the next round. Three frontier models (GPT-5.2, Llama-4-Maverick and a third frontier model), 18 homogeneous and 108 mixed conditions (one or two minority agents of another model, at announcement position 1, 5 or 2 and 4), 20 trials each, about 126,000 agent-rounds, 504,000 API calls, estimated cost $1,685 (their appendix D). The protocol is not about pricing; it is about whether announcements are honoured.

**Findings that matter for us.**
1. **Models read announcements differently.** In mixed Diner's Dilemma groups, a Llama minority agent among four GPT agents earned 0.82 against 2.37 for the majority (gap -1.55); among four agents of the third model the gap was -2.60. Their explanation: Llama treated announcements as coordination signals and complied, the others as cheap talk. A pair of GPT and the third model had no gap (both at the Nash payoff of 2.00). The gaps appear in round 0 and last all 10 rounds, and announcing last (position 5) made the gap wider, not smaller.
2. **It depends on the game.** Gaps are large in Diner's Dilemma, moderate in Public Goods (up to 0.45), and small (under 0.40) in Weakest Link, Volunteer's and El Farol. They explain: it needs a game where complying agents hand payoff to non-compliers. Bertrand undercutting is exactly such a game (the firm that does not hold the cartel price takes the market), so a mixed-family swap here could show the same asymmetry. That is a hypothesis, not a result.
3. **Deviations are mostly planned (self-reported).** When agents broke an announcement, the private plan had already said so (over 90 percent in the highest-deception conditions), but the same model ranged from 0 to 98.6 percent breaking across games. They note the plan text may not reflect internal processes; our message text has the same limit.
4. **Deception is not a fixed trait.** Four patterns over rounds: stable high deception, rapid learning towards honesty, gradual decay, and increase. For us: do not generalise a model's behaviour from one game; the pricing game is our only game, say so.
5. **Their stated limits:** three models, six games, five-agent groups, 10 rounds, self-reported plans; rates should not be read as estimates for deployed systems. Same style of limits for ours.

**What we can borrow (all exploratory, none enters the pre-registration).**
- **Newcomer-minus-incumbent payoff gap** as an exploitation measure after a swap (their "payoff asymmetry"): cheap, from data we already log. Report next to the collusion index, per swap type.
- **Immediate-effect window.** Their gaps appear in round 0 of mixed play. Look at rounds 31 to 35 separately from 31 to 60 (already planned as the early window, Eschenbaum notes).
- **Message-price consistency** (communication on): when a message names a price, compare it with the sender's price that round (their commitment breaking) and with the receivers' next prices (their announcement compliance). A regular expression on the message is enough; no private-plan stage (that would triple the calls).
- **Message order does not apply to us:** our firms reply simultaneously and see last round's messages, so there is no announcement order to vary.
- **Wording for the report:** they test mixed groups from round 0, like Keppo et al.; none of the papers replaces a member of an established group. Their finding supports expecting a different-family newcomer to read the incumbents' messages differently from a same-family clone, which is why the family swap is reported next to the clone.
- **Starter repo:** `LLM-Trust-Breaking` has no licence (recorded earlier): read for ideas only, copy nothing.

## 3. Risks the papers show us
- **Duration.** Keppo et al. ran up to 1,000 periods with a 32-billion-parameter reasoning model, 48 to 72 hours per run; Fish et al. ran 300 periods. We run 60 with 8 to 14 billion-parameter models and thinking off. Collusion may not form in time. That is what the pilot gate tests, and the fallback exists for it.
- **Prompt sensitivity** (Fish et al.): small wording changes move prices a lot. Our results are about our prompt; say so, and log any change.
- **Mixed-model groups behave differently on their own** (Shi et al., Keppo et al.). Always report the different-family swap next to the same-model clone.
- **Different game.** Both LLM pricing papers use logit demand (Calvano-style); ours is a simple homogeneous Bertrand game with integer prices. Comparisons to their price-elevation percentages are not like for like. Report our index only.
- **Starting point for novelty.** Keppo et al. (heterogeneity fixed from the start) is the closest work; Shi et al. cite Tewolde et al. 2026 (CoopEval), which reports cooperation deteriorating when co-players vary. Now read: it re-matches strangers every round and does not replace one member of a stable group (see `05_COOPEVAL_ANALYSIS.md`), so the novelty wording stays "to our knowledge".

## 4. Expectations written before any data (exploratory, not part of the pre-registration)
1. **Undercutter:** collusion breaks (positive control; if it does not, the harness or the index is wrong).
2. **Clone with wiped history:** the new agent starts from the rules alone and may price near the competitive level at first; the other two may punish or may pull it up. Direction uncertain; this is the main unknown.
3. **Different family:** similar to the clone if the new agent's prices differ from the cartel's focal price; smaller damage if the family shares the same price-war caution that Fish et al. found.
4. **Hidden vs announced:** announcing a replacement should increase suspicion and lower prices more than hiding it, if the agents react to the message at all.
5. **Two of three replaced:** larger effect than one of three.
These are guesses to be tested. If the data disagree, report the data.

## 5. Text we can use in the report (own words, with citations after full reading)
- Motivation: pricing agents are being delegated pricing authority and updated or swapped over time; existing LLM collusion studies fix the agents for the whole run; we ask what happens when an agent is replaced after a cartel has formed.
- Framing sentence: "collusion persistence under agent replacement, as a stylised model of model updates" (already in the plan).
- Limitations to state: short horizon, small models, one game, one prompt, integer price grid, no real-market claim.
- Dual-use appendix: the setup shows conditions under which an AI cartel is robust to turnover or detectable after it; say what it does and does not establish.
